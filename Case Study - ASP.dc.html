01 Research

Why the existing tools weren't working

01

Entirely manual, paper-based process

No tooling existed at all — every Biological Indicator cycle was tracked on paper. Cycles often ran 8+ hours, spanning a shift handoff: the morning staff started a cycle, and evening staff had to pick up and finish the notes, with no shared record between them.

02

No real-time status visibility

Staff had to physically walk to machines or make phone calls to check if cycles were running or complete.

03

Fragmented compliance records

Cycle history lived in multiple disconnected systems, forcing manual log reconciliation before every audit.

04

Low tech fluency, high error rate

Cycles were run by clinical staff, not technologists — limited technical familiarity meant missed cycles and incorrectly recorded notes were common.

Core Design Question

How might we design a connected app that enables healthcare professionals to manage sterilization cycles confidently — in a complex, regulated, high-stakes environment?

02 Users

Three personas, one shared need

SPD Technicians
Need instant cycle status at a glance, immediate alerts on faults, and a simple minimal-step flow. Clinical staff, not technologists — with cycles running 8+ hrs across shift changes, they need a handoff record that carries over automatically instead of relying on handwritten notes passed to the next shift.
Infection Control Managers
Need searchable full cycle history, one-tap exportable reports, and anomaly detection & flagging. Previously reconciled paper logs by hand before every audit, with no way to catch a missed or misrecorded BI cycle until it was too late.
OR Nurses
Need to know when instruments are ready, priority processing requests, and confidence in sterility status. Downstream of SPD's manual process, so any dropped note or delayed shift handoff directly delayed instrument turnover for surgery.

03 Approach

Research-driven, context-first

Contextual Inquiry

Observed SPD workflows across 3 hospitals — mapping every physical and digital touchpoint.

Users scan, not read — status states must be unmistakable within 1 second of viewing.

Stakeholder Interviews

14 participants across all 3 personas — capturing mental models, goals, and workarounds.

7 redundant steps in the legacy flow could be collapsed into automated confirmations.

Information Architecture

Card sorting with users to define structure — reducing dashboard cognitive load.

Connectivity feedback is essential — hospital networks are inconsistently reliable.

Iterative Prototyping

3 rounds of usability testing in clinical environments before developer handoff.

Critical vs. informational alerts must be instantly, visually distinct — no overlap.
04 Principles

Six rules that governed every decision

01 · Clarity over completeness

Show only what matters in the moment. Technical parameters are accessible, never primary.

02 · Reduce cognitive load

Multi-step flows chunked into single-purpose screens — one question, one action.

03 · Safety first, always

Critical alerts surface immediately. Destructive actions require explicit confirmation.

04 · Context enables decisions

Every screen provides the minimum context needed to act confidently — no more, no less.

05 · Test in the real environment

Prototypes tested in clinical settings — with gloves, noise, and real time pressure.

06 · Compliance as a design input

Regulatory and accessibility requirements shaped the interface from the first wireframe.

05 Solutions

From 11 steps to 4

A persistent dashboard surfaces cycle status across all connected sterilizers — color-coded states, real-time progress, and push alerts — so technicians never have to leave their station to know what's happening.

07 Learnings

What I took away from this

Domain immersion is essential

Generic UX patterns don't work in healthcare — deep workflow understanding builds trust.

Hierarchy impacts safety

Visual clarity directly affects decision-making and reduces risk of errors.

Design for unreliable connectivity

Offline support and graceful degradation are critical in hospital environments.

Test in real context

Real-world testing (noise, pressure, constraints) reveals issues missed in controlled settings.

Compliance drives better design

Regulatory requirements can shape clearer, more structured interactions.

Prototypes accelerate alignment

Interactive prototypes resolve stakeholder ambiguity faster than documentation.

"Designing for healthcare taught me that clarity is not a preference — it is infrastructure for patient safety."
